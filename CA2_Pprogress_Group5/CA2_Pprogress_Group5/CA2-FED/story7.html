North Korea ⇆ South Korea

One Flag, Two Koreas

Two nations still technically at war marched into the opening ceremony under one flag, and skated as one team.

Quick Facts

When
February 2018
Where
Pyeongchang, South Korea
Who
North and South Korean athletes and officials
Remembered for
A joint march under the Korean Unification Flag and a combined women's ice hockey team

The Korean War never formally ended. The 1953 armistice stopped the fighting but was never followed by a peace treaty, which means North and South Korea have technically remained at war for over 70 years — separated by one of the most heavily fortified borders on Earth, with almost no contact allowed between ordinary citizens on either side.

In early 2018, in the run-up to the Winter Olympics in Pyeongchang, the two governments agreed to something that hadn't happened in over a decade: North and South Korean athletes would march into the opening ceremony together, under a single blue-and-white Unification Flag instead of either country's own. Alongside that, they fielded the first unified Korean Olympic team in 66 years — a joint women's ice hockey squad, training and playing side by side.

For a few weeks in Pyeongchang, two countries that couldn't agree on almost anything else agreed to skate as one team.

It wasn't simple. Decades apart meant real differences in dialect, training, and even hockey terminology, and the decision was controversial in South Korea, where some athletes felt it cost them their own Olympic spots. But on the ice, the players built something real in a matter of weeks that their governments hadn't managed in a generation.

The unified team didn't medal, and the diplomatic thaw that followed didn't last much longer than the Games themselves. But for that brief stretch, two nations that share a language, a history, and a border they can't cross walked out together anyway.
