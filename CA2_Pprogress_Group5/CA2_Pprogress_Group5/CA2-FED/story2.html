United States ⇆ Soviet Union

Apollo-Soyuz: A Handshake in Orbit

At the height of the Cold War, an American and a Soviet spacecraft docked in orbit, and their commanders shook hands through the open hatch.

Quick Facts

When
15–17 July 1975
Where
Earth orbit, about 225 km up
Who
3 NASA astronauts & 2 Soviet cosmonauts
Remembered for
The first joint US–Soviet spaceflight, and the handshake that sealed it

For most of the 1960s, the United States and the Soviet Union raced each other to space as an extension of the Cold War — Sputnik, Gagarin, the Moon landing, all of it scored like a rivalry. By the early 1970s, though, a period of easing tensions known as détente opened the door to something neither side's rocket program had ever attempted: doing it together.

The two countries' spacecraft weren't built to connect — different atmospheres, different docking hardware, different everything — so engineers designed a special adapter module just to let an American Apollo capsule and a Soviet Soyuz capsule link up in orbit. On 17 July 1975, they did. Apollo commander Tom Stafford and Soyuz commander Alexei Leonov opened the hatch between their two spacecraft and shook hands, live on television, 225 kilometers above a planet still very much divided.

Three hundred kilometers above a divided planet, two crews who'd trained for years to beat each other instead reached out and shook hands.

The crews spent nearly two days together — sharing meals, swapping gifts, running joint experiments, and doing interviews in each other's language, since both crews had spent months learning enough Russian and English to talk to one another without translators. Stafford's Oklahoma-accented Russian reportedly amused Leonov enough that the Soviet crew joked they were speaking a new language: "Oklahomski."

The mission didn't end the Cold War, and the two space programs went back to mostly working apart for another two decades. But it planted the idea that former rivals could share a spacecraft — and it's a straight line from that handshake to the Shuttle-Mir missions of the 1990s and the International Space Station that followed, where American and Russian crews have lived and worked together continuously ever since.
