Türkiye ⇆ Syria ⇆ 90+ Nations

One Disaster, Ninety Nations

When the ground split open in southern Türkiye and northern Syria, rescue teams arrived from all over the world — including neighbours who hadn't spoken to each other in decades.

Quick Facts

When
6 February 2023
Where
Southern Türkiye and northern Syria, centred near Gaziantep
Who
Search-and-rescue teams and aid workers from more than 90 countries
Remembered for
Old rivals — Greece and Armenia among them — showing up within days despite decades of closed borders and cold relations

On the morning of 6 February 2023, a 7.8-magnitude earthquake tore through southern Türkiye, followed hours later by a second, almost as powerful. Tens of thousands of buildings came down in seconds, many while people were still asleep inside them. The damage reached across the border into northern Syria, already worn down by over a decade of war. It became one of the deadliest natural disasters of the century.

Within hours, rescue teams were on planes. Over the following days, more than 90 countries sent people: sniffer dogs and handlers from Mexico, structural engineers from Japan, field medics from India, firefighters from the United States — strangers who had never worked together, digging side by side through the same collapsed buildings, often with their bare hands once the machinery couldn't reach.

For a few weeks, borders that had stayed shut for generations opened for people they had never met.

Some of the responses were quietly remarkable. Greece, which has clashed with Türkiye for generations and came close to war as recently as the 1990s, sent one of the very first rescue teams within a day — echoing a similar gesture after a 1999 earthquake that had briefly thawed relations between the two countries. Armenia, whose land border with Türkiye had stayed closed since 1993 over an unresolved historical conflict, opened it for the first time in decades to send trucks of aid straight across.

Rescue crews kept working around the clock through freezing winter nights, pulling survivors out of the wreckage sometimes days after the quake. Photographs like this one — a young girl freed alive from the rubble by rescuers who had no shared language but the work in front of them — became a symbol of the response: proof that when the ground actually opens up, the lines drawn on maps tend to matter a lot less than who shows up.
